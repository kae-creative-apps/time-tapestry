# Preserving a heart of generosity

Product decisions, October 3, 2026.

## Conversation

Start with a remembered person or moment: “When you think about helping others over the years, is there a person or a story that comes to mind?” Follow what matters to the storyteller. Keep the existing four themes and maximum of two follow-up questions per theme.

Financial giving remains welcome. When it fits and has not already been covered, the interviewer may ask once: “Were there people or causes you supported financially that you would like your family to know about?” Hesitation, a wish to move on or an earlier answer are reasons not to ask again. Do not assume reluctance based on age.

Choose a relevant follow-up rather than working through a list:

- What made that matter to you?
- How did you decide where to give?
- What did being involved look like in an ordinary week?
- What, if anything, did you get to see come from it?
- What would you want your family to understand about why you gave?

Remembered years, regular commitments, time, hospitality and care are meaningful without a dollar total. Do not calculate lifetime giving, rank generosity or presume sacrifice from an amount. If an amount is volunteered, preserve its context and uncertainty. Do not infer beneficiary outcomes or repeat the interviewer's suggestions as biographical facts.

Faith language follows the storyteller's framing. Matthew 6:19-21 and 2 Corinthians 9:6-7 remain distinct paraphrases about heavenly treasure and willing generosity. They must not become an invented quotation or a promise of financial return.

## Quiet written notes

The third story's review includes an optional “Where you sowed” notebook. No added spoken interview step is required. It can preserve multiple people or causes in free text, how the person was involved, approximate years, meaning for the family and observed or reported impact. An amount or range is optional and separately disclosed. The person can identify it as one gift, regular giving or an estimate, without the app computing a total or declaring it independently verified.

Saving privately stores a separate owner-only notebook. It does not add source takes, revise a story, call an AI provider or generate media. Notes are excluded from recipient/requester views, interview context and automatic story/film/print inputs. This is application access control, not a claim of end-to-end encryption or secrecy from authorized operators and backups.

Choosing words to add opens an editable preview. Only an explicit add action copies those words into the story draft. The normal save, review and collection approval still apply. Later notebook edits do not retract or alter words already copied into a story. Private note changes use revision checks to prevent silently overwriting another tab's save.

No giving detail is selected automatically for printed postcards. The separate public-message editor and print consent remain the only route to printed copy.

## Why this approach

This is a design judgment grounded in narrator control and open-ended interviewing, not a tested claim about all older adults. The Oral History Association recommends open questions, sensitive listening, the ability to refuse or restrict topics and review before release: https://oralhistory.org/best-practices/. Test the wording with willing storytellers before claiming improved disclosure or comfort.

Design reference lock: keep Time Tapestry's existing PortalUI typography, espresso text, paper surfaces and sage optional panels. Clearful's guided journal suggests one prompt-led writing column; Medium's editor distinguishes saved drafts from publication. Borrow those interactions, not their colors or publishing language. Use visible labels, large controls, keyboard focus and a collapsed optional panel. Refero style search had no relevant result; the existing product is the visual target.

## Conversation rehearsal cases

- A person already described church giving in the faith theme: acknowledge the story without repeating the financial question.
- “I don't want to brag”: acknowledge the concern, offer meaning or the option to move on, and do not press for an amount.
- A person remembers decades of volunteering but no financial gift: preserve that story without implying something is missing.
- “About fifty dollars a month, I think”: preserve the estimate and cadence without deriving a lifetime sum.
- “I hope it helped children learn”: retain this as a hope, not a measured educational outcome.
- “I would rather write it”: offer the notebook once and continue without asking them to read private details aloud.

The prompt changes require a real voice rehearsal to assess tone. Automated privacy and save tests do not establish conversational quality.
